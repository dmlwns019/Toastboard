# *The MVP*
In other words, what should our small, “dumb” prototype be able to reasonably accomplish? The goal is to avoid most complexity while still ensuring that the prototype demonstrates the core idea. Let’s split what we need into three parts, for each main part of Toastboard.
1. ## Crumbs – Breadboard Modules
	- *The main defining feature of crumbs is that they give intuitive, visual feedback through LEDs on the current signal being passed through. Each crumb should be able to:*
		1. Be powered by either the toaster or another crumb
		1. Receive a signal and display the color, or display red if two signals are being passed through the same node
		1. Give a current through the top that components/jam can interact with
			1. The top current should also be able to connect to another node, which will then possess the same current 
	- However, the model is very basic. The enclosure will likely be made of cardboard (if an enclosure exists at all)
	- At the most basic level, each crumb will contain a microcontroller and RGB LED, with simple alligator clips for connections with other crumbs, jam, or the toaster
	- **Goal: 3 crumb modules**
1. ## Toaster – Main Brain
	- *The Toaster is essentially an arduino with a case over it, allowing easier access to digital pins. This will just be alligator pins connected to certain pins on the arduino.*
1. ## Jam – Components
	- *We will have input components and output components, which can be programmed using the toaster.*
		1. ### Input components:
			1. Button
			1. Potentiometer
		1. ### Output components:
			1. LED (single color)
			1. Servo motor
	- Each component will also feature a red LED that will light up if a module is placed incorrectly. However, this feature will not be included in the prototype version for simplicity’s sake.

# *Deciding the microcontroller for each crumb*

Each crumb will contain a microcontroller to manage inputs and the LED color. However, because there are many crumbs, we need a programmable, yet easy-to-use and affordable microcontroller. Here are some nominations:
1. ## ATtiny402 
	- Cheapest and tiniest
	- 8 pins, slightly limited
	- 4KB flash and 256B RAM
	- Limited memory, I/O can be restrictive as crumb functionality is expanded
	- ### Good for simpler crumbs
1. ## STM32C031 
	- Much more advanced microcontroller with better processing and RAM
	- 32-bit ARM processor with up to 48 MHz clock speed
	- 32KB flash and 12KB RAM
	- Better suited towards complex control, communication, or data processing tasks
	- ### High cost and complexity may be too much for early prototypes
1. ## ATtiny1616 
	- Small and relatively cheap
	- 20 pins provide lots of flexibility for expansion
	- 16 KB flash and 2 KB RAM
	- Still uses 8-bit AVR architecture, development is relatively simple
	- ### Good balance of simplicity, cost, and capability 

All things considered, the **ATtiny1616** is probably the best option for early prototyping. It’s a *good balance between capability and efficiency*, considering the strict budget present. Although a singular ATtiny402 is cheaper than a singular ATtiny1616, the limited memory and pins will eventually be outweighed by required functionality, making it unviable for a long-term project. On the other hand, the STM32C031 is strong when considering long-term flexibility, due to its high memory and capability. However, even if we were to ignore the budget limits of the first prototype, a kit of crumbs where each crumb contains a STM32C031 will likely be too expensive. 

To allow for easy debugging and flexibility when creating the crumb prototypes, I figured it would be easier to use a breakout board containing the ATtiny1616. This way, I can freely experiment and debug the system before designing a custom ATtiny1616-based PCB around the finalized design. 

The breakout board selected is the Adafruit ATtiny1616 Breakout with seesaw - STEMMA QT / Qwiic
